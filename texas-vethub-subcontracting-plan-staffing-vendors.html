VetHUB Subcontracting Plans in Texas: A Guide for Primes and Staffing Vendors

What Texas’ shift from the HUB program to VetHUB means for a state-agency bid: when a subcontracting plan is required, what a good faith effort looks like, and what to verify before you name a staffing subcontractor.

October 27, 2026 · 8 min read · Hearthstone & Co. Workforce Solutions

The short version: if you are bidding on a Texas state-agency contract that is expected to include subcontracting, the solicitation may require a VetHUB subcontracting plan, and a response without one can be treated as non-responsive. The plan either shows you will self-perform the work or documents a good faith effort to subcontract with VetHUBs. The program changed sharply in the last year and is still in litigation, so this guide sticks to what the Comptroller, the statute and the agencies publish, and tells you where to check for the current answer.

What changed: from HUB to VetHUB

For years, Texas state agencies used the Historically Underutilized Business (HUB) program to push contract dollars to certified small businesses. The Comptroller of Public Accounts now runs the program as VetHUB, short for Veteran Heroes United in Business. Its stated aim is to certify service-disabled veterans and increase VetHUB contracts and subcontracts with the State of Texas.

The Comptroller’s FAQ says the VetHUB rules took effect on May 12, 2026, replacing emergency rules effective December 2, 2025, and that the Comptroller “revoked all businesses previously certified based on race, ethnicity or sex,” with an exception for businesses that proved ownership and control by service-disabled veterans. The FAQ adds that applications previously submitted are being reviewed for eligibility under the new rules.

The change is contested. The NAACP Legal Defense Fund’s explainer reports that a lawsuit was filed in March 2026, that a state district court issued a preliminary injunction on April 14, 2026 covering the plaintiff businesses, that the state has appealed, and that a trial was set for November 9, 2026. That is an advocacy organization’s account, not a court record, and the outcome could change what applies to a bid. Treat everything below as the program’s rules as published on October 27, 2026, and confirm with the Comptroller before you rely on it.

Who qualifies as a VetHUB

The Comptroller’s FAQ says certification requires that at least 51 percent of the business be owned, managed and operated by Texas resident owners who are service-disabled veterans. The owner must have at least a 20 percent service disability, as identified by the federal military department. The business must be primarily based in Texas and meet SBA size standards in 13 CFR 121.201.

That is narrower than “veteran-owned.” An SBA VOSB or WOSB certification, or a veteran-business listing from another body, is a different program and does not stand in for VetHUB certification. A prime should look for the VetHUB listing itself. The Comptroller describes its VetHUB directory as a list of all active VetHUBs certified through the statewide program.

When a subcontracting plan is required

The requirement comes from Texas Government Code § 2161.252. An agency that considers a contract with an expected value of $100,000 or more must, before it solicits bids or proposals, determine whether there will be subcontracting opportunities. If it finds that there is that probability, it must require each response to include a subcontracting plan, and a response that lacks one “must contain a plan to be considered responsive.” The Comptroller’s FAQ adds that an agency may decide not to require a plan if it concludes VetHUB subcontracting is unlikely, and that it documents that decision in the procurement file.

In practice, you do not have to guess. The solicitation says whether a plan is required and attaches the form. The Comptroller’s forms page lists the Subcontracting Plan Form along with supplementary forms for the good faith effort and for subcontracting opportunity notification, and a progress assessment report used after award.

The two paths inside a plan

Per the Comptroller’s FAQ, a plan documents “self-performing or good faith effort to subcontract with VetHUBs.” The proposed rule text published in the Texas Register in March 2026 describes the same choice as the solicitation method or the self-performing method. That proposal was published before the rules took effect, so read the adopted rule if the exact wording matters to you.

  • Self-performing. You state that you will do the work with your own workforce.
  • Good faith effort. You identify the portions of work you intend to subcontract and reach out to VetHUBs that can do them. The FAQ says primes must notify two VetHUBs for each subcontracting opportunity. The March 2026 proposal describes written notice to at least two HUBs per opportunity, down from a former minimum of three, sent at least seven working days before the response is submitted.

Individual agencies and universities publish their own working guides on top of the state form. Texas State University’s internal VetHUB guide, revision 9/2026, is one readable example. It has the prime list every portion of work it intends to subcontract with a description, a code and a percentage, complete a separate section for each opportunity, contact at least two qualified certified VetHUBs per opportunity when available, give them at least seven business days, and keep the notices and any responses. If fewer than two qualified VetHUBs are available, it asks for documentation of the search. It also says plainly not to treat meeting the participation goal as a substitute for the good faith effort. Your agency may differ, so follow the instructions in your solicitation.

What happens after award

A plan is a commitment, not just a bid attachment. Under Texas Government Code § 2161.253, the contractor must make good faith efforts to implement the plan, and an agency that determines the contractor failed to do so may bar it from further contracting opportunities with that agency. The Comptroller’s FAQ also notes that an agency may reject a plan that was not made in good faith or was left incomplete, and that a VetHUB listed in the directory at the time of the good faith effort is treated as a HUB for evaluating the plan even if it later loses status.

Using a staffing subcontractor: a prime’s checklist

If part of your scope is clinical staffing, such as RNs, LVNs and CNAs, a staffing firm can be one of the subcontracting opportunities. Before you name one in a plan:

  • Look the firm up in the VetHUB directory yourself. Do not rely on a letter, a logo or a statement in a capability deck. Record when you checked.
  • Match the scope to the opportunity. Describe the staffing portion the way your solicitation form asks, and confirm the firm actually provides that type of work.
  • Keep the paper trail. Save the written notice you sent, the date, the recipients and every response. The same file covers you if an agency audits the effort.
  • Check the clinicians, too. A subcontractor’s certification says nothing about who staffs the shift. Ask how it screens, credentials and monitors exclusion status; our credentialing questions guide and OIG and SAM screening post cover what to request.
  • Confirm how the workers are engaged. W-2 versus 1099 changes who the employer is. See our W-2 vs. 1099 guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is VetHUB, and how is it different from the former Texas HUB program?

VetHUB stands for Veteran Heroes United in Business. It is the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts program that certifies service-disabled veteran-owned businesses and aims to increase VetHUB contracts and subcontracts with the State of Texas. The Comptroller says it revoked certifications that had been based on race, ethnicity or sex, except for businesses that proved ownership and control by service-disabled veterans. The program is also in litigation, so confirm its current status with the Comptroller.

Who can be certified as a VetHUB?

According to the Comptroller’s FAQ, certification requires that at least 51 percent of the business be owned, managed and operated by Texas resident owners who are service-disabled veterans with at least a 20 percent service disability identified by the federal military department. The business must also be primarily based in Texas and meet SBA size standards. Being veteran-owned, or holding an SBA VOSB or WOSB certification, does not by itself make a business a VetHUB.

When does a Texas state agency require a VetHUB subcontracting plan?

Under Texas Government Code § 2161.252, an agency considering a contract with an expected value of $100,000 or more must decide, before it solicits bids or proposals, whether there will be subcontracting opportunities. If subcontracting is probable, the agency must require a subcontracting plan, and a response without the plan is not considered responsive. The solicitation itself tells you whether a plan is required.

What counts as a good faith effort?

The Comptroller’s FAQ says a plan documents either self-performing or a good faith effort to subcontract with VetHUBs, and that primes must notify two VetHUBs for each subcontracting opportunity. Agencies can reject a plan that was not made in good faith or was left incomplete. Procedures and deadlines vary by agency and solicitation, so follow the form and instructions that come with the solicitation.

Is Hearthstone & Co. a certified VetHUB?

No. Hearthstone & Co.’s VetHUB application is in progress, and we have not been granted VetHUB certification. Until certification is granted and the business appears in the Comptroller’s VetHUB directory, we should not be counted toward a VetHUB subcontracting plan. We do hold SBA certification as a VOSB and WOSB, which is a separate federal program.
