<p class="lead"><strong>The short version:</strong> Choosing a staffing vendor and onboarding one are different jobs. Onboarding is the paper trail: a signed agreement, insurance certificates, a credential file for each clinician, and proof that exclusion screening happens and keeps happening. Have it all before the first shift, because a surveyor, an auditor or your own compliance officer will ask for it after something goes wrong, not before.</p>
<p>If you are still deciding between vendors, start with our <a href="/staffing-agency-vetting-scorecard" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">staffing agency vetting scorecard</a> and the <a href="/blog/what-to-ask-staffing-agency-credentialing" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">credentialing questions to ask an agency</a>. This guide picks up after you have chosen.</p>
<h2>1. The vendor agreement</h2>
<p>Most facilities have a standard staffing or vendor agreement, and most agencies bring their own. Whichever paper you use, read it for the topics below. This is a checklist of subjects to cover, not legal advice; your counsel decides the terms.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scope and roles.</strong> Which credentials the vendor will supply (RN, LVN, CNA, or others) and what each may be directed to do. Our post on <a href="/blog/rn-vs-lvn-vs-cna-snf-staffing-mix-texas" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">RN, LVN and CNA staffing mix</a> explains why the duties belong in the request.</li>
<li><strong>Credentialing and screening standard.</strong> What the vendor must verify before assigning anyone, how often it re-screens, and what proof it gives you.</li>
<li><strong>Employment status.</strong> Whether clinicians are the vendor's W-2 employees or independent contractors. Our guide to <a href="/why-w2-staffing-for-healthcare-facilities" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">W-2 staffing for healthcare facilities</a> covers why this matters to you.</li>
<li><strong>Insurance and indemnification.</strong> What the vendor must carry and what it must indemnify (see section 2).</li>
<li><strong>Cancellation, no-shows and replacements.</strong> Notice windows, what happens when a clinician does not arrive, and how a replacement is requested.</li>
<li><strong>Conversion to your payroll.</strong> Whether and how you can hire a placed clinician directly.</li>
<li><strong>Timekeeping and invoicing.</strong> Who approves hours, in what system, and when disputes must be raised (see section 4).</li>
<li><strong>Term and termination.</strong> How either side ends the arrangement, and what documents the vendor returns or retains.</li>
</ul>
<h2>2. Certificates of insurance</h2>
<p>Ask the vendor to have its insurer issue certificates for the coverages your agreement requires, and keep them with the agreement. Which coverages, limits and additional-insured terms you require is a decision for your risk manager and counsel, so we do not suggest numbers here. Two practical habits: put the expiration dates on a calendar so a lapse does not go unnoticed, and ask for a fresh certificate at each renewal rather than assuming coverage continued.</p>
<p>If the vendor employs its clinicians on W-2, workers' compensation coverage is one of the items to ask about, because it determines who answers for an on-the-job injury. At Hearthstone &amp; Co., our W-2 clinicians are covered by Hearthstone's workers' compensation; ask any vendor, us included, to document its coverage in writing.</p>
<h2>3. The credential file for each clinician</h2>
<p>The agreement says what the vendor must do. The credential file shows that it did. For each person assigned to your facility, request the following, or confirm you can view it on request:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>License verified at the source.</strong> For RNs and LVNs, that means the Texas Board of Nursing. The Board gives employers <a href="https://www.bon.texas.gov/employers.asp.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">e-Notify</a>, which it describes as a free system that informs you of discipline or licensing alerts for the nurses you enter, plus a disciplinary and imposter alert page. We walk through the lookup in <a href="/blog/verify-texas-nursing-license" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">how to verify a Texas nursing license</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Nurse aide status.</strong> For CNAs, Texas HHSC says providers <a href="https://www.hhs.texas.gov/business/licensing-credentialing-regulation/long-term-care-credentialing/nurse-aide-registry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">can verify employability with the Employability Status Check Search</a>, and that using it is the equivalent of using the Nurse Aide Registry. HHSC also notes it no longer provides written verification, so keep a dated record of your own check.</li>
<li><strong>Exclusion screening results.</strong> See section 5.</li>
<li><strong>Background check and drug screen results,</strong> or the vendor's written confirmation that they were completed and when.</li>
<li><strong>Health and immunization documentation</strong> your facility policy requires of anyone entering resident or patient areas.</li>
<li><strong>Education and work history, and references,</strong> as your policy requires for employed staff.</li>
</ul>
<p>The federal <a href="https://files.asprtracie.hhs.gov/documents/healthcare-facility-onboarding-checklist-fillable-final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">Healthcare Facility Onboarding Checklist</a> from HHS's ASPR TRACIE is a useful model. It was written for hospitals and other facilities that need to bring in surge staff quickly, and its first phase covers the same ground as this section: verifying licenses and certifications, background checks, drug screening, health history and immunizations, and reference checks. Treat it as a template to adapt, not a regulatory requirement for your building.</p>
<h2>4. Orientation, competency and timekeeping</h2>
<p>Credentials answer whether someone is allowed to work. Orientation answers whether they can work safely on your unit. The same ASPR TRACIE checklist separates three layers: administrative onboarding, facility orientation (emergency procedures and codes, incident reporting, key locations, HIPAA, IT and EHR access, badging) and unit-specific orientation (unit safety, verifying unit competencies, equipment training, medication administration access, chain of command, shift handoff and downtime documentation).</p>
<p>Decide in writing which of these the vendor documents and which your facility completes before or during the first shift. Then settle the plain operational items that cause the most friction later: who approves hours and in what system, how badge and EHR access is requested and removed, and who the vendor's scheduling contact is.</p>
<h2>5. Exclusion screening: ask for proof, not assurances</h2>
<p>Exclusion risk reaches facilities that use contracted staff. The HHS Office of Inspector General's <a href="https://oig.hhs.gov/exclusions/special-advisory-bulletin-and-other-guidance/the-effect-of-exclusion-from-participation-in-federal-health-care-programs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">guidance on the effect of exclusion</a> makes several points that bear directly on vendor onboarding:</p>
<ul>
<li>Providers are not required by statute or regulation to check the exclusion list, the LEIE, but OIG recommends checking "prior to employing or contracting with persons" and periodically afterward.</li>
<li>OIG recommends applying the same analysis to contractors, and singles out services integral to patient care, such as nurses provided by staffing agencies.</li>
<li>If you rely on a contractor's screening, OIG recommends that you validate that the contractor is actually conducting it.</li>
<li>OIG updates the LEIE monthly and says screening employees and contractors each month best minimizes overpayment and civil monetary penalty liability.</li>
<li>OIG describes civil monetary penalties for each item or service furnished by the excluded person, plus an assessment and possible exclusion of the facility. It also says a facility that reasonably relied on a staffing agency to screen, and used due diligence to confirm the agency was doing so, may reduce that exposure.</li>
</ul>
<p>Put together, the onboarding request is straightforward: ask the vendor for its screening policy, for the result for each assigned clinician before the first shift, and for a way to see that it repeats monthly. Exclusion records are searchable on OIG's LEIE and on <a href="https://sam.gov/content/exclusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">SAM.gov</a>; our <a href="/blog/oig-sam-screening-clinicians" style="color:var(--em);font-weight:600">OIG and SAM screening guide</a> covers the databases in detail.</p>
<h2>A one-page request list</h2>
<p>Send this to a new vendor before the first assignment:</p>
<ol>
<li>Signed agreement covering the topics in section 1.</li>
<li>Certificates of insurance for each coverage your agreement requires, with expiration dates.</li>
<li>For each clinician: source-verified license or nurse aide status, background and drug screen confirmation, health and immunization documents, and references.</li>
<li>Written exclusion-screening policy, plus each clinician's pre-assignment result and the monthly re-screening schedule.</li>
<li>A named scheduling contact and an escalation contact.</li>
<li>Your orientation and competency plan, with the split of who documents what.</li>
<li>Timekeeping and invoicing process.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Do we have to check the OIG exclusion list for agency staff?</h3>
<p>The HHS Office of Inspector General says providers are not required by statute or regulation to check its exclusion list (the LEIE), but recommends checking before employing or contracting with a person and periodically afterward. OIG applies the same logic to contractors, including nurses provided by staffing agencies, and says that if you rely on the agency's screening you should validate that the agency is actually doing it.</p>
<h3>Is a copy of the nurse's license enough?</h3>
<p>A copy is a starting point, not the verification. The Texas Board of Nursing offers employers e-Notify, a free notification system that alerts you to discipline or licensing changes for the RNs and LVNs you enter, and a disciplinary and imposter alert page. For nurse aides, Texas HHSC says providers can verify employability with its Employability Status Check Search, which combines the Nurse Aide Registry and other checks.</p>
<h3>What should a certificate of insurance request include?</h3>
<p>Ask the vendor to name the coverages your agreement requires and have its insurer issue certificates showing them. Which coverages, limits and additional-insured terms you require is a decision for your risk manager and counsel, not a fixed rule. For a vendor that employs its clinicians on W-2, workers' compensation coverage is one of the items to ask for.</p>
<h3>Who is responsible for orientation and competency of agency staff?</h3>
<p>Plan for it as a shared task and settle it in writing. The ASPR TRACIE onboarding checklist, a federal planning tool built for surge staffing, separates administrative onboarding, facility orientation and unit-specific orientation, including verifying unit competencies and equipment training. Decide which of those the vendor documents and which your facility completes before the first shift.</p>
<h3>How is this different from evaluating a staffing agency?</h3>
<p>Evaluating asks whether a vendor is the right fit. Onboarding is the paperwork you collect once you have chosen one. Our vetting scorecard and our credentialing-questions guide cover the evaluation; this guide covers what to have on file before the first shift.</p>
